## Summary

> [!info] Why is this resource worth keeping?
> Notes from the University of Sheffield TRAM (2023/24) development programme session **"Mentoring and Coaching"**, held 03/11/2023 and led by Dr Esther Allen (Researcher Development Manager). The session covers the differences between mentor, coach and manager roles, the concept of the "Manager as Coach," coaching questioning techniques, and two structured coaching models (GROW and OSCAR).

- Useful as a reference for the skills, framing and structured questioning techniques behind coaching/mentoring conversations, and for the "manager as coach" management style.

## Key Takeaways

> [!tip] The essential insights from this resource
> Distill the most important points. What would you want to remember if you came back to this in 6 months? Use progressive summarization, bold the critical phrases.

- **Session objectives**: understand the roles of mentor, coach and manager; understand the purpose of coaching/mentoring in an organisational context and the role of the "Manager as Coach"; understand the skills, behaviours, attitudes, beliefs and values of an effective coach/mentor; understand contracting and the process to effectively coach or mentor.
- **Role definitions explored**:
  - **Mentor** — encourages/empowers another in their professional/career development; a developmental activity **driven by the mentee**. May involve working holistically to build confidence/reflection/a development plan, sharing personal experience, and offering an impartial, non-judgemental "extra-player" in someone's network.
  - **Coach** — a position/role of support; a **two-way collaborative** process between coach and coachee. May involve collaboratively identifying areas to develop, guiding toward goals, and teaching critical-thinking skills that build self-reliance.
  - **Manager** (old-school framing, flagged as "quite a simplified view") — a position of power/authority overseeing others' work: delegating, giving feedback, monitoring performance, making decisions, handling conflict.
  - **Coaching vs Mentoring comparison**: Coaching is short-term (up to 6–12 months), performance-driven, agenda co-created by coach and coachee, with specific/measurable outcomes. Mentoring is longer-term (a year or more), development-driven and holistic, with the **agenda set by the mentee** and the mentor supporting it, and a broader interest in overall development rather than specific measurable results.
- **"Manager as Coach"**: coaching is a recognised management style with significant crossover with **servant leadership**. Being a good coach is increasingly seen as a requirement of being a good manager. Reflections shared from manager-coaches described it as improving listening, building trust in working relationships, encouraging more proactive career-development conversations, and stepping back to let the team own problem-solving.
- **Why avoid always giving directive advice**: you don't know as much about the person's situation as they do; hearing your suggestion can halt their own thinking; over-reliance creates dependency (managers aren't always available); it risks a power dynamic; people are more likely to follow through on their own ideas; there isn't always a single right answer. Directive advice still has its place though — it's quicker, useful when the coachee lacks information, can prevent mistakes/wasted time, and is sometimes simply necessary (e.g. deadlines).
- **Group discussion — skills/characteristics of a manager-coach**: active listening; allowing the coachee to make their own decisions rather than "I'm always right"; empathy; spotting anxiety/avoidance; confidence to communicate directly; less instructive/more collaborative; balancing directive vs collaborative approaches.
- **Group discussion — challenges**: coachee not engaged/taking advice on board; sometimes a directive approach is necessary (e.g. deadlines); setting expectations. Jon's own notes added: coaching is a **slower process** than telling someone what to do; balancing "developing staff" against "getting the job done" and other organisational demands; hard to maintain the approach under stress/pressure; building sufficient trust for the employee to fully engage.
- **Traits of a good manager-coach** (Jon's notes): respectful of others' opinions, open to honest discussion, open to challenge/change, avoids passing judgement, gives and listens to feedback, accepts the (slower) process, recognises the limitations of the role and that it's about more than "telling someone what to do."
- **Group discussion — benefits/limitations of a manager-coach**:
  - To the **employee**: more support (vs task-focus only), job satisfaction, ongoing development rather than annual/6-monthly reviews, faster progression, tailored/individual approach — but some employees just want to be told what to do ("Yoda conversations"), and coaching doesn't suit everyone.
  - To the **organisation**: increases engagement, improves individual and organisational performance, helps identify/develop/retain high-potential staff, improves collegiality — but is a slower process than some management styles, can cause deadlines to slip, and requires investment in training managers as coaches.
- **Establishing a coaching/mentoring relationship**: set aside time and an appropriate location; put the mentee in the driving seat (ask what they want/need); listen and explore motivations/needs; support real, specific goal setting (mentee-led or collaborative); avoid being too directive to build problem-solving skills/resilience; build trust and rapport through supportive challenge; encourage and enthuse; build accountability (set intentions/expectations); clarify what confidentiality means to both parties. **Coaching and mentoring require the other person's permission/engagement — you cannot coach someone who doesn't want it — and it is not a "cure-all" for poor culture or systemic issues.**
- **Coaching-style questions**: open, non-directive prompts (e.g. "How are things going?", "What's working well for you?", "What does a 'good' look like?", "What's the first thing you're going to do towards [objective]?"). Notably **no "why" questions** — "why" can imply blame or judgement. Ask questions you're genuinely interested in the answer to; get comfortable with silence while the other person thinks; use feedback to check on the relationship.
- **Coaching models** (optional, not mandatory to use):
  - **GROW**: Goal (what do you want?) → Reality (where are you now?) → Options (what could you do?) → Will (what will you do?).
  - **OSCAR**: Outcome (goals of the session) → Situation (team member's current situation/feelings) → Choices (generate/weigh options) → Actions (define next steps) → Review (plan for reviewing progress).
- **Encouraging reflection**: other reflective models exist (Gibbs cycle, Portsmouth Pathway, Kolb's learning cycle). A four-part reflective cycle was shown: Activity (what happened? — facts) → Reflection (what did I experience? — feelings) → Conceptualization (why did that happen? — findings) → Application (what do I do next? — futures).
- **How to be a great coach/mentor**: put the mentee/coachee in the driving seat; establish clear boundaries/limitations; remember it's a mentee/coachee-led relationship requiring their openness; hold confidential, non-judgemental, open conversations; ask for and give feedback; communicate, reassess and adapt. How you follow up matters less than making sure you do (verbally, email, Google Form, etc.).

## Summary

- Useful as a structural checklist for each CfS section, a source of official EPSRC links, and a worked walkthrough for planning the Impact content of a proposal.

## Key Takeaways

**Letters of Support (LoS) — "hot off the press" update (31 Aug 2021)**
- Project partners cannot normally receive funding directly from the grant. Two exceptions: (1) a partner providing services/equipment via a formal procurement process audited by the host research organisation; (2) small amounts for travel/subsistence to attend project meetings, requested by the PI and fully justified.
- A well-written project partner LoS should confirm the organisation's commitment and identify: the value/relevance/possible benefits of the work to the partner; the period of support and full nature of the collaboration; how the partner adds value. Where relevant: projected market size/customers/sales, and how the partner will commercialise the technology beyond the project.
- Allowed **up to 3 Letters of Support / Partners**.
- Source: https://www.ukri.org/councils/epsrc/guidance-for-applicants/what-to-include-in-your-proposal/project-partners-letter-of-support/

**General EPSRC guidance**
- Always check the EPSRC website for the latest — "What to include in your proposal": https://www.ukri.org/councils/epsrc/guidance-for-applicants/what-to-include-in-your-proposal/ — this is **definitive** info from EPSRC and covers Coronavirus impact, overview, Case for Support, justification of resources, project partners' LoS, equipment, national importance statement examples, multi-proposal projects, IP protection, health technologies impact/translation toolkit, and flexible working. This session mostly addresses the **Case for Support** (the other 6 pages of the CfS, after the Track Record).
- For IPR — a plan should be in place locally (not necessarily written into the proposal), because reviewers may ask about it and you need to be able to state the arrangements if a reviewer raises it.
- Reviewer forms and guidance: https://epsrc.ukri.org/funding/assessmentprocess/review/formsandguidancenotes/
- Standard grants scoring guidance: https://epsrc.ukri.org/funding/assessmentprocess/review/formsandguidancenotes/standardgrants/

**Structure of "Description of proposed research and its context"**
Five components: Background; Research hypothesis and objectives; Contribution to knowledge; Programme and methodology; National Importance.
- **Timeliness** can be included here too — why now?
- Make section headings **explicit** on the page — sub-headings make it easy for reviewers/panel members to find the information they need.
- **Start with an Exec Summary** — begin the narrative with the overview. The exec summary can sit before the Track Record but provides a nicer flow into the Case for Support if placed *after* the Track Record.

**Background (~1.5 pages)**
- Introduce the proposal topic and its academic, industrial, policy, societal, or other relevant context.
- Indicate who might benefit from the research, and how.
- Explain where the work fits in the current portfolio of EPSRC-funded research and how it relates to past/current work in the UK and abroad — **must** tackle how it relates to EPSRC funding, current opportunities, themes, funding interests and recently-funded grants; highlight what's relevant to your proposal.

**Background and Related Work**
- What is known to the community — include important work you are actually building on.
- What is not known to the community — critique fairly and with appropriate language.
- Why what is not known is worth knowing — **this final point is often missed off but is really important**. Link also to your track record if you've been active in the area.
- Include references to work by experts in the field who may end up reviewing your proposal — acknowledge their contribution and how you'll build on it.

**Know the Priorities — EPSRC Strategic Themes / EPSRC Themes**
- Themes change slowly (they're strategic) — always check the latest.
- Themes diagram/list included: [[AI|Artificial Intelligence]] and Robotics, Digital Economy, Energy, Quantum Technologies, Global Uncertainties, Healthcare Technologies, Living With Environmental Change (LWEC), Manufacturing the Future, Research Infrastructure, Engineering, Information and Communication Technologies, Physical Sciences, Mathematical Sciences.
- Link: https://epsrc.ukri.org/research/ourportfolio/themes/
- Know the priorities of EPSRC and the Manufacturing field specifically.

**Contribution to Knowledge** — same three-part structure as Background: introduce topic/context; indicate who benefits; explain how it fits EPSRC's current portfolio and relates to past/current UK and international work.

**National Importance**
- Contribute to the health of other research disciplines; to current/future UK economic success; to future development of key emerging industries; or address key UK societal challenges.
- Meet national strategic needs by establishing/maintaining unique world-leading research activity, including niche capability.
- Fit with and complement other research in the UK portfolio and EPSRC's portfolio and strategy.
- Workshop exercise: 5 minutes in groups on "where can you go to / what can you refer to, to establish National Importance?"
- **[[Made Smarter]] is always a good reference**: https://www.ati.org.uk/publications-tools/publications/ — even though the [[Made Smarter]] Review is 4 years old, the problems it identifies still exist.
- "So who wants it?" — cite reports from authoritative/influential bodies (e.g. The Royal Society, [[Rolls-Royce]], The Royal Academy of Engineering, Microsoft Research, IBM) and statements from UK or EU Government and their agencies. Relevant policy/strategy references noted: Industrial Strategy (now focused much more on Energy and Manufacturing), MadeSmarter, Healthy Ageing Initiatives, Net Zero Carbon, Levelling Up/Build Back Better, R&D Roadmap, PM's 10 Point Green Plan.
- Check the age/relevance of any policy documents cited — have they been replaced or superseded? Check with experts in the field.

**Research Hypothesis and Objectives**
- Set out your research idea/hypothesis.
- Explain why the proposed project is **novel and timely**, e.g. emphasising scientific ambition or potential transformative outcomes.
- Identify the overall aims and **measurable objectives** against which outputs, outcomes and impacts will be assessed.

**Programme and Methodology (~2.5 pages)**
- Detail and justify the research methodology; describe the work programme including research and impact-related activities; identify the contribution of each team member, including project partners and stakeholders; provide objectives and milestones to monitor progress and explain how the project will be managed.
- **Impact is now part of the main Case for Support**, so must be included here.
- Can include planned outputs, e.g. that a paper will be produced and a specific conference targeted.

**Why proposal writing is hard (facilitator's — "JC's" — opinions)**
- We're trained in the scientific method — more comfortable communicating authoritatively about technically provable matters than persuading people to speculatively fund something. **A grant proposal is not a research paper.**
- We usually communicate with fellow domain experts, but proposals have a more general (panel) audience too — put yourself in their shoes, as you do (or should) when teaching.
- Reframe "selling": it's "writing a persuasive evidenced-based case that renders apparent to a specialist and more general scientific audience why the problem needs urgent attention, why what you propose is an ambitious assault on this problem with great potential for academic influence and/or wider societal impact, and why you are the ideal person or team to do it."
- You're writing against template/page-length constraints — treat this as a challenge to fit everything in intelligibly, not a grievance. We live in an era of Research Council research priorities and should appreciate that. The proposal process is a (biased) slightly stochastic process, and you can do things to load it in your favour.
- **"The Logic of Failure"**: don't take rejection personally ("They rejected it → they rejected me → I am a failure"); two unsuccessful proposals can breed gloom — **you must prepare assuming you can be funded**, and **use reviewers' comments to improve future proposals**.
- "It's a lottery" is a cynical misconception — serious statistical analysis would not support that hypothesis, though some people are mind-blowingly lucky.
- "The World of MORE": the motivation for a proposal often comes from the organisation, not just yourself; proposal writing competes with teaching, admin, pastoral support, supervision, recruitment, conference/journal papers, etc. Don't throw in speculative proposals that aren't written with success in mind.
- Despite the difficulty, **EPSRC's process is comparatively lightweight** vs. many funders internationally (e.g. 100+ page NSF proposals) — "reason to be cheerful."

**EPSRC — Flexible Working**
- EPSRC lets you tailor requested support, e.g.: part-time working for PI/co-I/PDRAs/technicians/wider team costed on the grant; support for costs above standard care arrangements so the PI/team can attend grant-related activities (e.g. conferences) where costs can't be met by the employer; support for other adjustments/adaptations due to personal or health circumstances.
- You do **not** have to describe the personal circumstances behind a flexible working request — just describe the support required. Seek local Departmental or RIS advice.

**EPSRC — Responsible Innovation**
- EPSRC has a duty of care to promote responsible innovation, initiating ongoing reflection about potential ethical and societal implications, going beyond ethics, public engagement, risk and regulation.
- Applicants are expected to work within the EPSRC Framework for Responsible Innovation: https://epsrc.ukri.org/research/framework/

**Reviewers and Benchmarking**
- Reviewers give an overall score **1–6** (1 = "scientifically or technically flawed"; 6 = "very strong application that fully meets all assessment criteria"). Moderation panels are asked to focus on the **comments** rather than the scores.
- Link: epsrc.ukri.org/funding/assessmentprocess/review/formsandguidancenotes/standardgrants

**Heilmeier questions** (credited to Heilmeier — anyone proposing research/product development should be able to answer):
- What are you trying to do? Articulate objectives using **absolutely no jargon**.
- How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?
- What's new in your approach, and why do you think it will be successful?
- Who cares?
- If successful, what difference will it make?
- What are the risks and payoffs?
- How much will it cost? How long will it take?
- What are the midterm and final "exams" to check for success?
- No jargon is a recurring theme — it also appears explicitly in EPSRC's own advice.

**"Some comments" — Simon Peyton-Jones and Alan Bundy**
- Your CfS will, with luck, be read by one or two experts in your field — but the programme manager and most panel members won't be expert, so you **must** write for their benefit too.
- Programme managers/panel members see tens or hundreds of cases for support — you have **one minute or less** to grab the reader's attention.
- Ask lots of people to help improve your proposal; get it reviewed by experienced grant writers/winners before submitting.
- Make sure the **first page acts as a stand-alone summary** of the entire proposal.
- Their "Questions to Ask" checklist: Does the proposal address a well-formulated problem? Is it a research problem, or a routine application of known techniques? Is it important, with useful effects if solved? Is special/urgent funding actually necessary rather than normal lab resources? Do the proposers have a good idea to base the work on? Does the proposal explain clearly what work will be done, what results are expected, and how they'll be evaluated? How would you judge success? Is there evidence the proposers know related work? Do they have a good track record of research and publishing?
- Their "Why Proposals Fail" list: industry ought to do it instead; no evidence proposers will succeed where others failed; insufficient technical detail to judge a claimed new idea; proposers seem unaware of related research; the work already appears to have been done; proposers attempting too much for the funding/timescale; too expensive for the probable gain; the proposers' institution should be funding it. (More examples in the slide deck on Blackboard.)
- Advice on writing/submitting proposals is widely available online — the process is fairly similar worldwide.
- **Have your proposal reviewed — everyone agrees with this.** Further tips (slightly dated, refers to "Pathways to Impact" but the gist still holds): https://www.ukri.org/councils/epsrc/guidance-for-applicants/what-to-include-in-your-proposal/overview-of-information-needed-for-your-proposal/

**"Demonstrating support for Impact in your Research Proposal" — Tijana Close and Sam Garwood, University Impact Officers, Research Services**
- What is Impact? Intentionally broad definition: **Change** (in behaviour, understanding, feelings, decisions, processes, strategy, perspectives, debate, products, services) **AND Subsequent benefits** (to health, wealth, happiness, environment, economy, knowledge, etc.) — but **it must be outside academia**.
- UKRI/ESRC expect: explaining how research plans enable anticipated impacts; including space and funding for events; ensuring user participation/inclusion as co-investigators; considering skills/skills development of team and partners; considering evaluation measures; making use of university resources. "The strongest proposals we receive indicate a mind-set towards impact in which research, knowledge exchange (KE) and impact are intertwined" (ESRC).
- Good applications are concise, detailed, concrete, honest, realistic, imaginative, specific to the project. Weak applications are rambling, generic, vague, unrealistic, "bolted on," overambitious, unrelated to the proposal.
- You are **not** expected to deliver world-changing impact, be solely responsible for delivering it, or accurately predict what it will be. You **are** expected to give it thought, correctly identify what impact might occur however modest, take reasonable steps toward it, and ask for funding to do it.
- Common misconceptions: there's no fixed list of acceptable impact activities; **you own your impact** — it's as personal as your research.
- Academics should engage with impact at the application stage (think early, plan for it, consider appropriate activities), throughout the project (deliver activities, stay open/flexible, build continuous review in, build relationships), and after the project ends (follow-on funding, evidencing, sustainability).
- **Workshop framework (4 steps), with where each maps in the CfS:**
  1. *Why are you interested in this research?* (≤100 words: what question/problem, **not** how/methodology) → Background (context); National Importance (impact focus).
  2. *Who else might have a stake in this?* (list stakeholders/beneficiaries, be inclusive/long-term/speculative, consider downstream beneficiaries, be specific) → Track Record (partners/stakeholders in co-design); Background (introduce beneficiaries).
  3. *What could change for them?* (brief description of potential impact per beneficiary group, noting different groups may experience different impacts) → Background; Research Hypothesis and Objectives (measurable impact objectives).
  4. *What can I do to help make this happen?* (Who do I need to approach — not necessarily the beneficiaries directly? When? How, to get best results?)
  - Stakeholder categories reference diagram (public sector, policy community, community & third sector, business community, international community, the public, researcher at centre) — source: www.publicengagement.ac.uk
- Types of impact activities: ensuring relevance/exploring possible impact; translating research; dissemination to stakeholders; "contingent and emergent" review — e.g. policy briefings, videos demonstrating the technology or explaining the research.
- Remember: put yourself in stakeholders' shoes; what's in it for them; tailor activities to their needs/constraints/calendars; don't reinvent the wheel (use "gatekeepers"); consider monitoring/measuring engagement and impact. Ask whether trade organisations exist for unfamiliar industries, and how you'll measure impact (ask project partners what they're looking for — consider including in the Letter of Support). → Programme and Methodology (describe impact activities); Management (resources/responsibilities).
- Next steps: whittle activities down; work out detail for each (who delivers it, what project stage, how often, what expertise/resources needed, cost, how to get stakeholders to engage); talk to relevant support teams; write the final document; run it by friends/colleagues/impact managers/research services.
- Common pitfalls: focusing on academic impact/beneficiaries (exceptions apply); lack of clarity about beneficiaries/benefits; no logical engagement/influencing route; vague activities (e.g. "we'll make a website"); focusing only on dissemination rather than knowledge exchange/pathways to impact; claims without evidence; copying from another application.
- DO include: specific details of proposed activities (dates, venues, names, website content); considered funding requests; impact plans that make sense for your specific proposal (integrated in CfS, as a separate work package, or via infographics).
- **Who can help** ([[University of Sheffield]] contacts/links): Faculty KE teams (https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/pre/collaborate); Departmental & Faculty Impact Leads/Managers; Public Engagement Team (http://www.sheffield.ac.uk/pre/public-engagement); Media Team (mediateam@sheffield.ac.uk); ORDA research data sharing (http://www.sheffield.ac.uk/library/rdm/orda); Kaltura multimedia platform (https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/cics/digital-media); Schools Outreach Team (http://www.shef.ac.uk/schools/team); RS Impact & IP Team (http://www.sheffield.ac.uk/rs/commercialisation) — for IP/patents/confidentiality queries; TUoS Corporate Comms also has a dedicated Impact Officer.
  - Contacts: **Sam Garwood**, samantha.garwood@sheffield.ac.uk; **Tijana Close**, t.close@sheffield.ac.uk
- Q&A note: asking a Project Partner to include impact specifics (rather than generalities) in their Letter of Support — what they want to gain, how they'll use outcomes, how their industry benefits — is encouraged: it shows co-creation. LoS have no length limit, so the CfS can reference impact and point to fuller detail in the LoS (this also prompts reviewers to actually read the LoS, since detail there is sometimes missed). Partners can often describe benefits to themselves better than the applicant can.
- **Homework**: start working on the Case for Support.

**PI Responses (rebuttals)**
- **Responses matter, even if scores are low** - handled well, they usually work in your favour. You typically get 5 working days to respond to referee reports.
- The panel discusses all proposals with both the referee reports and the PI responses in hand. At EPSRC panels there are often three speakers who comment on how well criticisms/disagreements between referees were handled; the panel then produces a ranked list which EPSRC uses to decide funded/unfunded (with a "borderline" zone in between).
- **If you can respond constructively you can climb the rankings** - a strong response can move you up; a dismissive/careless one can move you down.
- **You generally need at least 2 favourable reviews to reach panel**, at which point a response is needed/recommended.
- Official (neutral) term is "PI Response", not "rebuttal". Responses are typically **two pages**, nominally to address factual inaccuracies and explicit questions raised - but in practice most people also use them to respond to implied criticisms (e.g. "It is unclear from the proposal how the investigators intend to do X..." is really an implied request for more detail, whether or not it's phrased as a question).
- UKRI's own advice (17 Aug 2021): use clear headings (can group by issue rather than by reviewer, though this "depends" - Jon's view is that sometimes it's better to tackle one referee's comments alone, particularly if you want the panel to disregard that referee), refer back to specific parts of your proposal, use references to publications to add weight, only include figures if genuinely helpful, and don't waste space thanking reviewers or restating their comments at length.
- Types of referee comments range from simply seeking clarification through to a full "hatchet job" - colour-coded on a scale from green (fine) to red/pink (problematic). Notably, **"gushing but lazy" (unsubstantiated praise) is flagged as problematic too**, since panels don't like unsubstantiated comments even when in your favour.
- **General response strategy**: refer to your actual submission where possible ("As we say in our proposal section 3.2.1..."); treat "insufficient detail on X" as effectively a request for more detail regardless of whether it's truly missing.
- For opinionated-but-unsubstantiated criticism (e.g. "I don't think this approach will work" with no rationale), the recommended tactic is to point out the referee gives no rationale and to cite where your proposal (and other evidence) already supports your approach.
- For "wants a different proposal" comments, acknowledge the referee may have a point but argue your chosen goals/approach are independently justified and cite evidence (e.g. sector reports) supporting your goals.
- For "hatchet job"/extremist referees, sweeping assertions can often be carefully rebutted by pointing out they lack scope/applicability caveats.
- Claire H's advice: **keep responses calm, measured and factual - don't get personal.** Dismissive responses can move you down the rankings; strong, reasoned responses can move you up.
- The **panel does not re-review the application**; the response is written for the panel, not the original referee (the referee never sees it). **Always give evidence**, not just assertion.
- Sheffield RS google site for PI responses: https://sites.google.com/sheffield.ac.uk/eng-res-support/main-menu/pre-award-support/pre-award-tool-kit/pi-responses

**New Investigator Awards (NIA)**
- NIA supports individuals holding an academic lectureship (or equivalent) who have **not previously led an academic research group or received a significant grant** (usually defined as one including PDRA time, capital equipment, or exceeding £100,000 FEC). Aim is to establish new academics as researchers via pump-priming funding for an initial project.
- Projects should be **self-contained with clearly defined objectives**; typical duration 1-3 years matched to those objectives. Complex, multi-faceted projects with many objectives are not appropriate for this scheme.
- **This is not a fellowship.** Guideline funding covers 10-20% PI time (for undertaking their contribution to the research, managing the team/project, and career development), RA support, reasonable conference attendance (with additional costs potentially available for those with caring responsibilities), and capital elements.
- Duration should be 1-3 years matched to objectives; if a university provides additional PhD/PDRA time into the project, appropriate management time can be requested (but not supervision time for doctoral students).
- Equipment restriction: EPSRC was trialling paying 80% FEC for equipment over £10k until April 2022 (previously required at least 50% host contribution); equipment exceeding the OJEU limit (£138k) is not permitted.
- Eligibility FAQs: fixed-term appointments are eligible if criteria are met and you're recognised as a full academic by your institution; being employed by more than one UK university is fine; having been Co-I on a previous proposal is fine as long as this would be your **first proposal as Principal Investigator (PI)**. **You can only apply to NIA once.**
- Cannot request PhD student funding (even part-funded) on an NIA. Can use a DTA (Doctoral Training Award) but cannot charge anything to the grant itself; fixed-term contracts are fine provided they extend beyond the grant; being CI on another grant is not a problem, since NIA just needs to be the first application **as PI**. Allowance can be made for career breaks etc. on a case-by-case basis - contact EPSRC to discuss.
- **Assessment criteria**: Quality, National Importance, Impact, Support from University, Ability to Deliver, Research Independence, Resources and Management - much as for usual EPSRC applications, but note the added emphasis on **Independence** and **Support from University**.
- Detailed EPSRC eligibility rules: investigators must be academic employees (lecturer or equivalent); fixed-term staff are eligible if the contract extends beyond the research end date and the host organisation gives normal permanent-employee support; **current holders of postdoctoral-level fellowships are not eligible** (e.g. EPSRC Postdoctoral Fellowship, Leverhulme Early Career Fellowship, Sir Henry Wellcome Postdoctoral Fellowship, likely also Leverhulme Trust Research Fellowship, Ser Cymru Fellowship, Daphne Jackson Trust Fellowship); staff employed on a research grant post are not eligible to be PI unless they hold an early career fellowship with equivalent stature to permanent staff (e.g. EPSRC Early Career Fellowship, EPSRC Open Fellowship, UKRI Future Leader Fellowship, Leverhulme Trust Major Research Fellowship, RAEng Research/Industrial Fellowship, Royal Society University Research Fellowship, Royal Society Dorothy Hodgkin Fellowship); applicants must be resident in the UK.
- Funding history nuances: previous holders of postdoctoral-level fellowships are welcome to apply if other criteria are met; previous holders of early-career-level fellowships are **not** eligible; current holders of early-career-and-above fellowships **including PDRA time** are not eligible, but those **not including PDRA time** are eligible; Co-I on an EPSRC grant is fine unless it was a significant workstream with PDRA time and significant input into the proposal; applicants who have previously directed the vision of a research group are not eligible - and case-by-case assessment applies if you led a project with more than 6 months PDRA time, capital equipment over £20k, or a single grant over £100,000 FEC. Overseas funding must be considered under the same principles.

**Institutional Statement (NIA)**
- Universities provide an Institutional Statement addressing: what appraisal process the proposal went through before submission; what support was given during development; fit with departmental strategy/priorities (even implicit ones, e.g. citing your REF 2014 Environment Statement or unwritten policies evidenced by appointment behaviour); how the applicant's expertise fits current/future departmental research strategy; how the proposal will help the applicant develop toward internationally-leading research; demonstrated ability to manage resources requested.
- Mentoring and training: do you have a mentor (get one if not - they can help with your proposal); does your university sign up to the Concordat for early career researchers or related frameworks (e.g. Vitae); what training is available departmentally/centrally (advice: "the centre" is often more enlightened about training than "the edges" - find out what's available regardless of it being your first grant).
- Appraisal/review process: **"IDEAS MUST BE YOURS!"** - guidance on presentation, process and requirements is fine, and significant review is encouraged (EPSRC would like all proposals reviewed). Evidence of quality induction into grant writing might include review by senior staff, head of research/group leader, researchers outside your group, academics at another university, or industrial collaborators.
- Resource management evidence: managing PhD students, previous grants (however small, including internal University grants), management/organisational responsibilities (e.g. budget holder for workshops), turning UG/MSc projects into papers - all demonstrate ability to turn resources into value.
- Support if the grant is successful, framed around: **you are an early career researcher, not a teaching & admin workhorse** - ECRs are often given reduced teaching & admin load (e.g. building to full load after year 3, common practice in the Department); a DTA award to extend the work after the grant is good (stipends = significant cash support); fee waivers (no stipend) can also give the research "legs" post-grant and are high value to the researcher; start-up funds; access to research group resources (e.g. travel funds); access to specialist equipment/compute facilities; being part of a well-funded laboratory (should apply regardless of grant outcome, but mention it if it helps the case); access to central resources for further development; a PhD position coming with a new post counts as (in a sense unconditional) support if aligned with objectives.

**Demonstrating Research Independence (for NIA)**
- Workshop organisation
- Programme committee membership
- Invited reviewer for a journal/conference
- Lead author on publications in a related field
- PI on a previous (small) funding award

**NIA - further advice**
- **Don't over-egg it**: be ambitious but practical. Seek advice from colleagues - ECRs tend to propose "a lifetime's work," which actually signals an inability to plan/assess workload.
- Aim to build a case for longer-term success - it's fine to show some interest in sustainability (e.g. one example cited: a researcher had already attended awareness/training days for ERC Starter Grants, even though not yet eligible until after completing the first grant).
- Build collaborations, e.g. trips within the UK to fellow researchers. Senior researchers often get requests for short visits - nothing is lost by asking, and it's in your interest to build relationships (but don't make cold/out-of-the-blue requests to people you don't know). Senior staff/groups are often willing to host, and it shows initiative to have arranged this.

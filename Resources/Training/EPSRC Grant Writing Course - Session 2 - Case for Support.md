## Summary

- Slides are University of Sheffield "EPSRC Advice" decks plus live workshop discussion/exercise notes; screenshots of slides are noted rather than transcribed where they carried little extra text.

## Key Takeaways

**Track Record - what it's for**
- A **CV is not the same as a Track Record** for a proposal. The goal is to convince the reviewer/panellist that you (your team) are **ideal for the specific proposed project** - not a general statement of eminence.
- EPSRC advice: use up to **two A4 sides** to show the team has the right mix of expertise/experience, especially for multi-disciplinary proposals. Should: summarise results/conclusions of applicants' recent EPSRC and non-EPSRC work; specify expertise at host/associated organisations and beneficiaries; detail past collaborative work with industry/beneficiaries; detail contribution to UK competitiveness or quality of life.
- Workshop exercise answers on what content to include: **output and impact of prior work**, **collaboration record** (with specific partners or generally), **previous funding success**, **publication record** (related to the proposal).

**Quality Research: Publications**
- Computer Science is more **conference-centric** than other disciplines - conferences are often taken as seriously as (or more than) journals; there's a "quasi-religious" conferences-vs-journals debate, but researchers rarely omit a *Science*/*Nature* paper even if only loosely relevant.
- Not all referees/panellists are expert in your direct topic - **explain why your publications should be judged high quality** rather than assuming name recognition of the venue is understood.
- Useful self-check exercise: identify your **1 top, 1 second-tier, and 1 third-tier journal and conference venue** in your field, and ask whether your research group colleagues would agree with your ranking.
- Publication status matters: "**Accepted for publication**" = real evidence of community acceptability (even before it's in print/available online). "**In prep**" is weak/unverifiable (could mean anything from "I like it" to "no one else has seen it") - **try to get work at least to "Under review"** rather than leaving it "in prep," especially if you'll have less time once a grant (e.g. First Grant) starts. **The state of your publication record affects WHEN you should submit your First Grant as PI.**
- "Finish it!" - finishing/publishing outputs is just good career advice (helps REF, promotion is based on publication quality - publication is *part of* research, not what follows it); a First Grant submission can provide extra motivation; fellowships often require citing your top 5 journal + top 5 other publications; state special circumstances (e.g. a career break) if applicable.
- **Prizes/awards**: conference best-paper prizes are probably the most likely source of recognition; even better if a best paper is later turned into a top journal paper.

**Citations and influence**
- Citations aren't just about absolute numbers - they're a function of **merit AND age** (of the paper and of you); referees know this, so demonstrate world interest in recent papers explicitly.
- Don't be afraid to quote **quasi-citations** (e.g. "our open source toolkit has been downloaded more than X times per year since Y and its use acknowledged in Z papers").
- A **narrative citation** ("Professor Big Cheese's group incorporated algorithms from my PhD paper") can carry more weight than a bare "30 researchers have cited [2]" claim. Be careful using "influential" without elaboration - a referee may check Google Scholar, Web of Science, or Scopus, so citations need to actually support the claim.

**Recognition and being a community player**
- Invited talks (plenaries at major/related conferences, to commerce/industry, local to the UK) and membership of influential bodies (government advisory groups, research council advisory panels, local community partnerships) all count.
- "You are in the business of making the best of what you have got" - **emphasise your strengths; don't mention weak areas** (legitimate given limited space).
- Personal note: for AMRC, get something in about what we do with the community.
- **Training and its relevance**: mention training that enhanced your ability to conduct/manage research, e.g. IPR/contracts negotiation awareness, entrepreneurship awareness training (Royal Academy of Engineering), Research Leadership programme (recruitment, team building, motivation, communication, strategic planning, outreach) - include if space allows and it adds to the case.

**Grants managed / New Investigator considerations**
- Avoid a bare list like "I got this whole series of grants [1,2,3,4,5]" - it shows you can sell ideas but **not that you can deliver quality research**. Much better: a specific narrative naming the grant, its value, and the outputs/impact/spin-off activity it produced (worked example given for a symmetry-breaking project).
- **For New Investigator applications**, if you lack a grants-managed record, use: internal projects managed, MSc/PhD supervision, staff management, turning managed projects into publications, internships, and conference committee roles - i.e. anything you have managed, guided, or supervised.
- **Collaborations**: show the investigative team is fit for purpose via a specific narrative (worked example of two collaborators' joint outputs feeding into the proposed work package). Collaborations "have to start somewhere" - if untested, make the case that this is a collaboration reviewers should want to see happen.
- **Ability to make things happen**: demonstrate initiative via workshops/competitions initiated, conference programme chairing, and local initiatives taken - again, make the best of what you've got where relevant.
- Sell yourself (quoting Andreas Zoeller, re: ERC grants but applicable elsewhere): **you need irrefutable evidence**, avoid unverifiable assertions, and create unique selling points.

**Tone**
- Aim for **firm, upbeat, and factual** - don't flannel, avoid loaded vocabulary, stick to specific facts and figures (rankings, prizes, citation counts) - but be careful with ranking claims since most rankings are somewhat controversial.
- Overall aim: "a calm, modest but firm, factual and positive **demonstration** of why you and your team are just the team for this proposal."
- EPSRC advice: explain any collaborations with visiting researchers (what they contribute, why they're the right person); if applicable, state where flexible working or a career break has impacted a team member - describe the **impact on track record/career development**, not the personal circumstances behind it.
- Institutional aspects to include where relevant: AMRC's deployed base of experimental infrastructure, kit highly relevant to the proposal (e.g. 5-tonne manufacturing robots), laboratory facilities, institutional subscriptions.

**Letters of Support (LoS) / Project Partners** (brainstormed by John Clark, Claire Hinchcliffe - Eng Faculty Research Services, and Peter Osbourne - AMRC)
- An EPSRC **Project Partner** is a collaborating organisation with an integral role in the research, contributing in-kind or cash (expertise, staff time, facilities, etc.). Project Partners **cannot receive funding directly** from the grant (except via a formal, audited procurement process for services/equipment), and cannot receive other funds such as travel/subsistence.
- Well-written LoS strengthen a proposal by showing the benefits of the collaboration and the research's potential impacts. Each letter should cover **why** the partner supports the proposal, **what** they will contribute, and **what** they hope to get out of it. Reviewers (and the panel, who see the whole submission) assess whether the collaboration is appropriate and whether routes/resources for knowledge exchange are identified.
- Contributions to quantify: cash, staff time, access to equipment, access to data, software, materials - different partners will often contribute different things.
- **You need to quantify/give a monetary value to contributions**, and consistency isn't easy: staff time is fairly easy to value, access to data trickier, software/facilities can be harder (commercial rates?) - the approach needs to be reasonable/defensible. This detail **goes in the J-eS form**.
- What partners might get out of a project (workshop exercise) included: access to upskilled staff.
- The overall aim is a **set of letters** collectively supporting the "why us" case, built on the Track Record.
- **Who writes it**: usually someone in the partner's management hierarchy, to show real commitment. **Who it's addressed to**: usually the PI, but can be wider for multi-university proposals, or the site lead if the partner supports work at a particular site.
- What else to include: a brief description of the organisation (don't assume referees understand what a specific sub-part of even a well-known company like Rolls-Royce does - name recognition isn't understanding); some key stats backing suitability/potential; previous track record of collaboration; evidence of engagement in preparing the proposal (e.g. brainstorming workshops).
- **Co-creation is a big theme within EPSRC (and UKRI)**. Regular company meetings are a good way to demonstrate co-creation - identifying a genuine need from the company through these sessions. LoS that reference a brainstorming session or regular meetings carry a lot more weight than ones that simply say the company is "interested."
- Jon's personal note from the session: "I need to engage companies to see if my proposal is actually a real need!"
- Companies may worry about formal commitment, but there are **no legal issues** here - proceed on the basis that the stated collaboration will go ahead as planned. Within the University, an LoS usually isn't needed, but sort out the internal politics first (e.g. equipment technically belonging to the University may not actually be available when you need it) - consider making internal partners a formal part of the proposal with staff input instead.
- **IPR**: you need an understanding of IPR, particularly around **pre-existing IPR**.

**Homework**
- Write a **draft Track Record** for yourself and your team for the project you have in mind.

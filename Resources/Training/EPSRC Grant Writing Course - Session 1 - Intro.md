## Key Takeaways

- **Speakers**: Prof. John Clarke (Comp Sci) covered EPSRC's remit/landscape and grant types; Claire Hinchcliffe (ex-EPSRC portfolio manager, now Royal Society faculty lead for engineering) covered the application/decision process.
- **EPSRC's place in the funding landscape**

Source: https://epsrc.ukri.org/research/ourportfolio/themes/healthcaretechnologies/strategy/toolkit/landscape/
  - **Why does EPSRC focus on TRL 1–3? "All to do with State Aid."**
- **State Aid**
  - The source included a "State Aid" slide screenshot: state aid is any advantage granted by public authorities through state resources on a selective basis to organisations that could distort competition/trade in the EU. Definition is broad — "an advantage" is anything an undertaking could not get on the open market.
  - See also: https://www.gov.uk/guidance/state-aid
  - Slide also referenced a document "State Aid in Research, Development & Innovation: A Guide for Universities" (AURIL & PraxisUnico; authors Christine Reid, Emyr Lewis & David Bembo) via a "Link" button — noted as "a useful link" (no URL visible in the screenshot itself).
- **Sectors vs Themes**
  - The source included screenshots of EPSRC "Sectors" and "Themes" bubble charts (sectors include Healthcare, Manufacturing, Energy, IT, Aerospace/Defence, etc.; themes include Manufacturing the Future, ICT, Physical Sciences, Engineering, Training, Non-Theme Specific, Energy, Research Infrastructure, Healthcare Technologies, Mathematical Sciences, Quantum Technologies, Digital Economy, etc.).
  - "Sectors are not always clear. You can overlap sometimes."
  - **"You don't really apply to a theme/sector, you apply to a panel. Sectors are the end users, themes are the funding streams."** Occasionally funding goes across panels.

- **Wider funding landscape**
  - UKRI ultimately funds all research (EPSRC, MRC, ESRC, BBSRC); also funding via industry partners like Microsoft Research, IBM, BAE, R-R, Siemens.
  - KTN also mentioned: https://ktn-uk.org/manufacturing
  - High-profile funders: RAE, Royal Society, Leverhulme Trust — the latter "funds almost anything and [is] worth checking."
  - EU funding is a large funder but "quite involved and require[s] a lot of organisation"; post-Brexit arrangements still evolving (as of the course date).
- **Types of EPSRC grants**
  - **Standard proposals**: can be created to fit a particular call (call indicates theme/scope; special panels convened; you compete against others in that field), or submitted in **"responsive mode"** — proposer free to suggest anything within the research council's remit, traditionally the mechanism for blue-skies/curiosity-driven research. All research councils must now show "impact" (not purely economic), judged in open competition against other responsive-mode proposals. JC noted he has sat on panels with 50+ applications to review in one day.
  - "Others": many variations on a theme exist in the grants world — "if there is something sensible you want to do then there is probably a funding route to pursue." Responsive mode can handle almost anything within remit.
  - **Personal Fellowships**: revolve around the proposed fellow setting their own research agenda for 3–5 years; great if you can get one but very competitive. Fellowships are about "creating evangelists and champions" for the research. Discipline-hopping awards exist for researchers wanting immersive experience in another field (e.g. for a year). Assessment leans heavily on track record (top journals/conferences, citations, career-stage-dependent) plus non-technical "fellowship material" criteria.
  - **New Investigator Award**: supports individuals holding an academic lectureship who have not previously led a research group or received a significant grant (broadly, one including PDRA time, capital equipment, or exceeding £100,000 FEC). Eligibility criteria must always be checked.
- **Decision process — referees and panels**
  - Proposals typically go to peer review by field experts, who write reports considered by a convened panel. Reviewer expertise varies from specialist to competent-in-general-area. EPSRC asks applicants to nominate referees and commit to using at least one nominated reviewer. Referees are busy people — proposals must be crafted with this in mind.
  - Referee reports vary hugely in quality/tone — from genuinely insightful to "infuriating." (Framed humorously as "Genius" vs "Idiot" referees depending on whether they liked the proposal.) Referees are typically "people like your supervisor... people like you."
  - **Rebuttals**: referees produce reports, applicants are informed of the comments, and with EPSRC you typically get 5 working days to respond — "responses matter." The panel meets with referee reports and applicant responses; two speakers (a non-expert and an expert) summarise the referee views and how well criticisms were addressed, producing a ranked list.
  - Ranking example given: proposals scored ~7.9–9.9 out of 10, split into "Fundable" (funded, or "second chance") vs "Unfundable" (unfunded) bands. **Actual score matters less than rank position** — closer to 10 is better. "Unfundable" means no amount of revision would get it funded. Where the funding cut-off actually falls depends on available budget; some near-miss proposals get a "second chance" (not guaranteed).
  - **Common reasons proposals aren't funded**: inappropriate track record (team must be justified as right for the proposed work; New Investigator Awards are aimed at those with little track record; fellowships require demonstrating future potential), not novel enough, unclear impact, incremental research, misaligned with the current call, not following application guidelines, risks/mitigations not considered (especially for adventurous proposals), too high a TRL target.
  - A recap exercise slide ("Exercise notes from last time") listed further reasons discussed in the group for proposals failing: out of scope of call, prescribed structure, impact not clear, resources not justified, personal antagonism toward proposers, over/under promising, unsatisfactory track record, sheer competition, luck, being trendy or untrendy, "academic hypocrisy," infeasible timescales/resources, not novel enough, weak value proposition, ethics issues (e.g. IP), "why isn't this funded by industry," being told someone else would do it better, jealousy, wrong kind of risk, losing the non-specialist reader, unfortunate terminology. Advice given: don't expect referees to look things up, and cite likely reviewers.
- **Components of an EPSRC standard proposal**
  - **Case for Support** — the technical core; track record (2 pages) + description of work (6 pages max).
  - **Justification for Resources** — why the requested resources are needed (2 pages max).
  - **Schedule** (e.g. Gantt chart) — fairly straightforward.
  - **Staff CVs** — typically 2-page max per named staff member (varies by call).
  - **JES-form** — the administrative interface to EPSRC: official finance figures, objectives, public summary, suggested referees, etc.
  - **Other supporting docs** — could be anything, e.g. letters of support.
  - **Pathways to Impact** — now retired as a standalone document; its issues are integrated into the Case for Support.
- **The EPSRC decision-process pipeline**: Admin Checks → Remit Checks → Reviewer Selection → Send to Panel? → Panel Member Selection → Panel Preparation → Panel Meeting → Funding Decision → Award Checks → Post Award Admin.
- **Principles of Peer Review (UKRI, based on EPSRC history)**: Expert Assessment, Transparency, Impartiality, Appropriateness, Confidentiality, Integrity and Ethics, ED&I (equality, diversity and inclusion), Separation of Duties.
- **Admin/remit/eligibility checks**: page counts, references, letters of support, alignment to sector/theme; lead council; lead theme (for standard mode); call scope; scheme eligibility (e.g. New Investigator Award); co-funding across councils/themes (where the novelty lies, best chance of success, proportion of co-funding, reviewer suggestions).
- **Reviewer & panel member selection**: EPSRC has a large pool of reviewers; applicants can nominate reviewers (avoiding conflicts of interest) and the portfolio manager will ask at least one nominated reviewer. Reviews exist to help the panel decide. "Send to panel?" is a decision point on whether the application (plus applicant's response to reviews) is strong enough to proceed. Panel members are drawn from the EPSRC "college" (reviewer pool), selected for technical expertise/topic coverage and diversity. A "good" panel member has knowledge of EPSRC processes, acts in the interests of "UK plc" (linked on the slide to the Principles of Public Life, no URL visible), and has a track record as a reviewer. The Chair is an experienced panel member/reviewer and effective facilitator.
- **Panel introducers**: read all assigned proposals plus as many others as possible — 1st introducer (generalist), 2nd (specialist), 3rd (generalist, contextual). They complete an introducer form highlighting reviewer agreement/disagreement, applicant/reviewer disagreement, score proposals against assessment criteria on a standard scale, and feed back on reviewer appropriateness/quality plus any applicant feedback.
- **EPSRC's role at panel**: introduce the process and wider theme priorities, ensure no re-reviewing, ensure EPSRC policies/procedures and conflict-of-interest handling, support the chair, keep a record of discussions and applicant feedback.
- **Panel process**: running order set by pre-meeting scores; introducers outline the proposal, summarise reviews/PI response, and lead discussion — **focus on comments, not scores; re-reviewing is not permitted**. Panel agrees an overall score (amendable in light of discussion), used to rank proposals for funding; at the end the panel reviews the order and assigns a quality cut-off. Panels may consider multiple lists (standard, New Investigator Award, etc.).
- **Q&A on panel fatigue** (Jon's question): Claire — that's the role of the chair/EPSRC at the panel; panel members are usually academics who want fairness because they'd want their own applications treated the same way, so discussion is generally fair. John — it's "high octane, but easy to stay awake"; there are breaks/lunch; lower- and top-scored proposals get less discussion time than borderline ones; the panel shouldn't overrule referees but should use their comments to decide.
- **Funding decision**: the budget holder decides the funding cut-off for each list, based on relative quality, financial deferrals, invited resubmissions, and panel feedback. The source included a screenshot of an "EPSRC Budget Allocations" flow diagram (Spending Review Budget → consultation with Council/SAN/SATs/Community → Delivery Plan → Council sign-off of expenditure by theme/mechanism → Expenditure Outputs by Theme & Mechanism, feeding a Theme Planning Process with SATs/EPSRC Strategy/PM Team → Theme Commitment Budgets → Theme Activity Budgets [Standard Research; Schemes/First Grant/Programme Grant; Research/Fellowships/Studentships] → aggregation toward a 4-year planning cycle), with quarterly commitment targets (±10%) and quality tensioning across activities within a theme budget.
- **Award checks**: finances, forms, and specific requirements are checked post-decision.
- **Useful tool**: "Grants on the Web" is worth checking for previously funded work — includes panel information and ranked lists of applications (only funded ones show detailed info/titles; others show only an application number).
